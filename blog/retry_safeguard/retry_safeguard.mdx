Retries are a double-edged reliability mechanism. When failures are transient, retries increase success rate. When failures correlate with saturation, retries can amplify load, worsen tail latency, and trigger a cascading failure commonly called a **retry storm**.

This post explains retry storms, then compares four retry safeguard patterns:

1. No retry
2. Retrying (unbounded, for example retry 3 times)
3. Retry budget (adaptive retry)
4. Retry circuit breaker (retry-only breaker)

The goal is not to argue that one pattern is always best, it is to provide a decision framework and evaluation metrics so you can pick the right safeguard for your dependency and traffic regime.

## What is a retry storm

A retry storm is a positive feedback loop:

- Downstream starts failing or slowing down.
- Callers retry.
- Retry traffic increases total load.
- Downstream saturates further, producing more timeouts and errors.
- Callers retry even more, and the loop escalates.

In practice, the storm becomes visible when **user traffic is flat but downstream traffic climbs**, accompanied by rising tail latency and utilization.

### The key metric: retry amplification

A useful way to quantify “storminess” is **Retry Amplification Factor (RAF)**, defined as:

- **RAF = total request sends / original requests**

RAF near 1 means little to no amplification. RAF near 3 to 4 typically indicates aggressive retry behavior during failures, and that is often the start of a storm.

## How to evaluate safeguard patterns

The following indicators are used to evaluate the safeguard patterns:

- Error rate (based on original requests)
- Tail latency (p95, p99 end-to-end)
- Server utilization (CPU, QPS, connection rate)
- Recovery time: the time it takes for the system to recover from a failure
- Retry amplification (RAF): the ratio of total request sends to original requests
- Error types (timeout, EOF, etc)
- Retry success lift (RSL): the percentage of requests that are retried and successful
- Breaker state metrics (open, half-open duration)
- Budget utilization (BU) for token buckets

The rest of this post uses these indicators to describe the tradeoffs of each pattern.

## Pattern 1: No retry

**What it is**

You send exactly one attempt per original request.

**Why teams use it**

- RAF is stable at 1.
- Downstream is protected from retry amplification.
- Behavior is predictable, and debugging is simpler.

**Where it struggles**

- You lose recovery for transient errors (short network blips, brief GC pauses, leader election hiccups).
- If your dependency is mostly healthy but occasionally flaky, success rate can suffer unnecessarily.

**What you should see in metrics**

- RAF: ~1
- Utilization: lowest among options
- Tail latency: often best during incidents (less self-inflicted load)

## Pattern 2: Retries (for example “retry 3 times”)

**What it is**

You retry on retryable errors up to N times, usually with backoff and jitter.

**Why teams use it**

- Highest potential recovery in transient failure regimes.
- Easy to implement and reason about locally.

**Where it breaks**

- Under overload or partial outage, retries can turn into load multipliers, and cause or worsen a retry storm.
- If many clients share similar retry schedules, they can synchronize and create “waves” of retries.

**What you should see in metrics**

- RAF: rises with failures, can spike aggressively
- Utilization: can increase sharply during errors (this is the dangerous coupling)
- Tail latency: can worsen rapidly under saturation
- RSL: often high in truly transient regimes, misleadingly low in overload regimes (many retries are wasted)

## Pattern 3: Retry budget (adaptive retry)

**What it is**

Retries are allowed, but within a **budget**, for example, “no more than 10 percent of the number of successful requests”. This is commonly implemented as a token bucket, which caps retry volume and prevents unbounded retry growth.

**Why teams use it**

- Preserves some recovery benefit while bounding RAF.
- Naturally “self-throttles” as success rate changes, because the budget is tied to success or a refill rule.

**Key tradeoff**

- In severe failures, you may see near-zero success via retries because the budget limits additional load (that is the point), but it can look like “retries do not help” unless you interpret it correctly.
- Budget accuracy matters, you need good accounting and windowing, especially during sharp traffic changes.

**What you should see in metrics**

- RAF: controlled, typically lower than unbounded retries
- BU: rises toward 100% during failure regimes
- Utilization: lower than unbounded retries, higher than no retry (by design, but capped)
- RSL: moderate in transient regimes, low in sustained overload regimes

## Pattern 4: Retry circuit breaker (retry-only breaker)

**What it is**

The client **disables retries** if the percentage of service errors exceeds a threshold (for example 80%). When the error rate drops below the threshold and stays below for a while, retries are resumed. Baseline traffic continues, only retries are blocked.

**How it differs from a normal circuit breaker**

- A normal circuit breaker cuts off requests (including original traffic) when a failure threshold is met.
- A retry circuit breaker cuts off only retry traffic, which avoids “all-or-nothing” request shedding while still preventing retry amplification.

**Why teams use it**

- Prevents retry storms while keeping baseline traffic flowing.
- Smoother than a full circuit breaker, fewer availability cliffs.
- Faster recovery than full circuit breaker, retries resume automatically when error rate improves.

**Where it struggles**

- Baseline traffic can still overload an unhealthy service, since you are not shedding original load.
- It may suppress retries during brief, blameless blips if thresholds and hysteresis are not tuned well.

**What you should see in metrics**

- RAF: drops sharply when the retry breaker is “open” (because retries are blocked)
- Breaker state metrics: visible open/close behavior
- Utilization: lower than unbounded retries during incidents, can still be high if baseline is large
- RSL: depends heavily on how often the breaker opens, but it is usually higher than retry budget in regimes where some bounded retry could still be valuable

## Side-by-side summary

| Pattern               | Main benefit                               | Main risk                                                 | Best fit                                                                  |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| No retry              | No amplification, simplest behavior        | No transient recovery                                     | Dependencies under frequent overload, or where retries are usually waste  |
| Retries (unbounded)   | Max recovery in transient failures         | Retry storms under overload, tail latency blowups         | Low risk dependencies, low concurrency, proven transient errors           |
| Retry budget          | Bounded recovery, caps RAF                 | Needs correct accounting, can limit recovery if mis-tuned | General-purpose default for large-scale systems                           |
| Retry circuit breaker | Blocks retry storms while keeping baseline | Baseline can still overload service, threshold tuning     | When you want “retry off during high-error” behavior, simpler than budget |
